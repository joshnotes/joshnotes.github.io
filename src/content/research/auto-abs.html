Securitization turns thousands of monthly car payments into rated bonds. It's one of the quieter corners of finance and one of the most important: U.S. auto loan ABS issuance reached a record $127 billion in 2025, the second record year in a row. This note walks through how the machine works and where this year's stress is showing up.

How a car loan becomes a bond

  1. Originate. A lender, often a carmaker's finance arm, a bank or a specialty lender, makes thousands of auto loans.
  2. Pool. It groups loans with similar characteristics into a pool.
  3. Sell to a trust. The pool is sold to a special purpose entity designed to be bankruptcy-remote, so investors are exposed to the loans rather than to the lender.
  4. Issue tranches. The trust issues bonds in layers. Senior tranches get paid first and earn the least; subordinate tranches absorb losses first and earn more.
  5. Add credit enhancement. Subordination, overcollateralization (more loans than bonds), a cash reserve account and excess spread (borrowers pay more interest than the bonds cost) all sit between losses and the senior bonds.
  6. Run the waterfall. Each month, collections flow down a fixed order of payments. A servicer collects payments and reports delinquencies, losses and prepayments.
  7. Watch the triggers. If losses or delinquencies cross set thresholds, cash gets redirected to protect the senior bonds.

The accounting side

I worked in securitization accounting at an auto finance company, and the part most explanations skip is the balance sheet question. Whether a transfer counts as a sale or stays on the lender's books as a secured borrowing depends on control and on whether the lender has to consolidate the trust under the variable-interest-entity rules. Many captive auto lenders keep their securitizations on balance sheet, so the loans and the bonds both stay visible in their financial statements.

Where 2026's stress is

Subprime auto delinquencies hit a record. Fitch's subprime 60+ day delinquency index reached 6.90% in January, the highest in data going back to the early 1990s. It eased to 5.80% by mid-year, still above the 5.60% a year earlier, while prime delinquencies sat at 0.48%. Subprime annualized net losses ended 2025 at 9.63%.

The stress is also concentrated. KBRA found that weaker borrower groups made up 37.4% of outstanding loan balances in 2026 but 81.5% of the 60+ day delinquency burden, so pool averages understate how badly the weakest borrowers are doing.

View

The structure is working as designed: senior tranches have deep protection, and the losses land on the subordinate tranches and the weakest pools, which is where they're supposed to land. The things to watch are trigger breaches in recent subprime deals and whether stress starts spreading from stretched borrowers into stable ones.
