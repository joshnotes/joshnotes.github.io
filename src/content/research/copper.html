Copper set a record of about $14,875 a tonne on the London Metal Exchange on September 10. By November 16, three-month copper had slipped to $13,210, the lowest since early July and about 10% off the high. Two days later it fell 4.6% in a day, its steepest drop in eight months, as the AI sell-off, a stronger dollar and worries about Chinese demand piled up.

The demand worry is real, but I don't think it changes the reasons copper set records in the first place, and those are mostly about supply.

Mine supply is growing slowly

New supply is coming mostly from expanding existing mines rather than building new ones, which is slower and smaller. Sucden Financial expects the market deficit to deepen beyond 2027 for exactly that reason.

Smelting fees fell to zero

Miners pay smelters a treatment and refining charge to turn concentrate into metal. When concentrate is plentiful, that fee is high. In 2026 the benchmark charge fell to $0 a tonne, the lowest on record, and spot charges turned negative as smelters competed for scarce concentrate. Chile's Antofagasta agreed a zero fee with a Chinese smelter for 2026. A fee of zero is about as clear a sign as this market gives that mined supply is tight.

Tariffs moved metal into the U.S.

U.S. tariff policy pulled copper into America. COMEX inventories climbed to a record near 700,000 tonnes, while LME and Shanghai warehouses together held barely 300,000. Refined copper was ultimately exempted from tariffs, but the risk of new ones keeps U.S. prices at a premium and metal parked where the rest of the world can't easily use it.

Demand

Grids, electric vehicles and data centers all need copper. In China alone, one estimate has State Grid investment rising more than 10% in 2026, adding roughly 320,000 tonnes of demand. The physical market there tightened even as prices fell this month: the Yangshan premium buyers pay for imported copper rose to a record of about $290 a tonne.

The balance

ING estimates a refined copper deficit of about 600,000 tonnes in 2026, after roughly 200,000 tonnes in 2025. Other forecasts are smaller, ranging down to around 150,000 tonnes, but most point to a tighter market.

View

Near term, copper trades with the macro mood: the dollar, rates and AI sentiment. Medium term, supply sets the floor. The signals worth watching are smelting charges, the COMEX-LME spread and the Yangshan premium. If smelting charges recover meaningfully, the supply story is loosening.
