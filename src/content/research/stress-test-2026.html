The Federal Reserve's 2026 stress test ran 32 large banks through a severe global recession: unemployment peaking at 10%, house prices down 30% and commercial real estate prices down 39%. All 32 stayed above their minimum capital requirements.

Most coverage leads with the capital ratio. I find it more useful to look at how the losses were paid for.

Revenue did most of the work

Pre-provision net revenue is what a bank earns before setting money aside for loan losses: net interest income plus fee income, minus operating expenses. In a stress test it's the first line of defense. Capital only gets touched once losses exceed it.

This year, projected PPNR came to $719 billion, or 3.0% of average assets. Projected losses were $708 billion. Cumulative pre-tax income over the nine quarters was about $1.2 billion, essentially zero. In other words, in a severe recession the banks' earnings almost exactly paid for their losses.

The aggregate common equity tier 1 ratio started at 12.8%, fell to a minimum of 11.2%, and ended the horizon at 12.7%, a peak-to-trough decline of 1.6 percentage points.

What moved the result

The Fed named three factors. Higher loan losses, from bigger balances and harsher scenario variables, pushed capital down. Smaller hypothetical rate declines meant smaller unrealized gains on securities, which also pushed capital down. But higher projected interest income, reflecting recent performance and those same smaller rate declines, more than offset both.

That last factor matters more than it looks, because the scenario's rate path is doing a lot of the work. A recession with rates falling less means banks keep earning more on their assets, which props up PPNR.

Where the losses come from

Loans account for $625 billion of the $708 billion, about 89%. Trading and counterparty losses add $37 billion. Credit cards are the single biggest source: roughly $200 billion of losses at a 17.1% loss rate.

What it means

This year's results don't change capital requirements. The Fed had already voted in February to keep stress capital buffers at 2025 levels until 2027, when the test will use loss models revised after public feedback. It has also proposed averaging two years of results to make the buffers less volatile. The sample grew too, from 22 banks in 2025 to 32 this year, so year-over-year comparisons need care. Banks raised dividends and buybacks soon after the release.

The Fed's Vice Chair for Supervision, Michelle Bowman, said the results underscore the strength of the banking system. I agree, with one caveat: the margin of safety comes from revenue as much as from capital, and that revenue depends on the scenario's assumptions about rates.

What to watch

The revised loss models in 2027, how sensitive projected PPNR is to the rate path, and whether credit card loss rates keep rising from here.
