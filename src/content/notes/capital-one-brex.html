When Capital One said in January that it would buy Brex, the corporate card and spend management company, most of the coverage was about the fall. The price, $5.15 billion, was 58% below the $12.3 billion Brex was valued at in early 2022, and it was hard to read about the deal without a little schadenfreude about that era's valuations. The deal closed on April 7, and Capital One has now reported two quarters with Brex inside it. That is enough to look at the deal from the buyer's side: what Capital One actually paid, what it got for the money, and how that price compares with what investors are paying for Brex's fastest rival, Ramp.

The price you read is not the price that was paid

The $5.15 billion was never a fixed number. Capital One paid roughly half in cash and half in its own shares, and the share half was set as a number of shares, 10.6 million, rather than as a dollar amount. In a deal built that way, the seller is effectively holding the buyer's stock from the day the deal is signed, so if the buyer's share price falls before closing, the seller gets less. Capital One's stock fell between January and April, and when the deal closed, the shares Brex's owners received were worth $1.9 billion rather than the roughly $2.4 billion they were worth at announcement. The cash came in a little lower too, after closing adjustments.

Measured against the 2022 peak, what Brex's owners actually received is 63% lower, not 58%. There is also a cost the headline leaves out. Right after closing, Capital One paid off $1.1 billion of Brex's debt, which is part of the cost of owning the business even though none of it went to Brex's shareholders.

Pricing a company is not the same as valuing it

The comparison everyone reaches for is Ramp, and it is a harsh one. Ramp raised money at a $44 billion valuation in June and reportedly at $60 billion in October, while Brex sold for about $4.5 billion. Before reading that gap as a verdict on Brex, it helps to separate two ideas. A valuation asks what a business is worth, given the cash it will generate. A price, including the price in a funding round, is what someone was willing to pay, and it depends as much on who is buying, how much they are buying and what everyone else is paying at the time. The simplest way to compare prices across companies of different sizes is to divide each by revenue.

Some of that gap is earned. Ramp was growing faster, close to 90% a year against Brex's 50%, and it had more customers: over 70,000 by June, against the 25,000 Brex served when the deal was announced. Faster growth deserves a higher multiple, and I would not argue that Brex should have sold at Ramp's. I don't think growth alone takes you from 6.5 times revenue to 30 times, though. The rest comes from the kind of transaction. Ramp's investors are buying small slices of a company that is still racing, at prices set by growth funds competing to get in. Capital One bought all of Brex, with control and every integration problem that comes with it. A whole company sold to one buyer will usually change hands at a lower multiple than a slice sold in a crowded round, so I read the Ramp number as the market's price for momentum, not as the value Brex was cheated out of.

Why Brex is worth more inside a bank

From Capital One's side, the logic is about funding. A card business earns the gap between what it collects on balances and fees and what it pays for the money it lends. On its own, Brex carried $1.1 billion of debt, and the customer cash it held, reportedly around $13 billion, sat at partner banks and money market funds, where Brex earned a fee rather than the full spread. Capital One has $475.8 billion of deposits to lend from. On the third-quarter call, Richard Fairbank said the integration would be selective, to protect Brex's momentum, and that the first benefits are lower funding costs and referrals from Capital One's existing business customers. Paying off Brex's debt on the first day is that idea in its simplest form.

The accounting shows how much of the price rests on that bet. By my arithmetic from the purchase price table in the 10-Q, about $3.1 billion of the $4.5 billion was booked as goodwill: the part of a purchase price that no identifiable asset backs, and that stands for what the buyer expects the business to become. Two thirds of what Capital One paid is a bet on future growth. So far the costs are visible and the benefits are small. Capital One has reported $143 million of Brex integration expenses across the second and third quarters, Fairbank warned at announcement that Brex would dilute earnings at first, and in the third quarter management described Brex's effect on card purchase volume as a modest tailwind.

My take

I think the 58% markdown says more about 2022 valuations than about Brex. Brex was growing again, had turned cash-flow positive in October 2025 by Sacra's account, and sold for about 6.5 times revenue to a buyer that can fund its cards more cheaply than it could. For Brex's late-stage investors, that is a loss. For Capital One, I think it is a fair price for a business it can make more profitable than Brex could on its own, and a cheap one if Brex keeps growing anywhere near its old pace. The risk I would watch is that Brex slows down once it is inside a bank, the people who built it drift away, and the goodwill gets written down a few years from now.

What would change my mind

If Brex's growth falls well behind Ramp's for several quarters, or Capital One writes down any of the $3.1 billion of goodwill, the price stops looking cheap. If Capital One starts reporting that Brex is growing faster inside the bank than it did on its own, I would call it a bargain.
