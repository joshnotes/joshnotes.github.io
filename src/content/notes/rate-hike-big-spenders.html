Everything I read this week comes back to the same thing: the Fed is going to raise rates again in December. Futures put the odds above 90% after October's hot inflation print, and the minutes from the October meeting showed almost every official expecting more hikes. The usual logic is simple. Borrowing gets more expensive, so companies spend less. So I started wondering about the biggest spenders out there right now. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta are on track to spend more than $720 billion on data centers this year. Does a quarter point even register for them?

My gut says no, and I tried to figure out why.

Who's actually putting up the money?

A few years ago these companies paid for everything out of their own cash. Microsoft still can. Amazon, Alphabet and Meta are now spending more than their businesses bring in, so they're going to outside investors. The hyperscalers have sold more than $370 billion of bonds this year. Meta has lined up a forward sale of about $28.7 billion of its own stock. And private credit funds like Blue Owl are financing whole data center campuses through joint ventures that keep the debt off the big companies' balance sheets.

What strikes me is who's on the other side of those deals: people betting that AI will throw off enough cash down the road to make all of this worth it. If you believe a data center will earn 15% a year, do you care that your cost of money went from 9% to 9.25%? I don't think you do. And if you think it'll only earn 7%, a rate cut wouldn't save it either. The Fed controls the risk-free part of the rate. The rest, the premium investors charge for taking the risk, comes from how much they believe in those future cash flows.

The numbers seem to back this up. If every dollar of those bonds cost a full quarter point more, that's roughly $0.9 billion a year in extra interest against $720 billion of spending, which feels like a rounding error to me. And the price of this money has already gone up by a lot more than a quarter point. A year ago Meta sold a 30-year bond at 5.625%. This month it sold one at 7.5%, almost two full points higher, and investors still put in orders for nearly four times what Meta was raising.

So where does the hike actually bite?

I don't think rates are irrelevant here. I just think they hit something other than the spending. The first thing is the stock price. Most of the payoff from a data center is years away, and the further out a cash flow is, the more a higher rate shrinks what it's worth today. By my math, a quarter point takes about 0.2% off a dollar due next year but about 4.5% off a dollar due in twenty years. Then again, the 10-year yield is already up about a full point this year, so the bond market has done most of that damage already.

The second is the layer underneath the big names. The developers building the data centers that Meta and Microsoft lease often borrow from private credit, and a lot of private credit is floating-rate, so a hike reaches them right away. The Fed's own staff flagged this in October, warning that off-balance-sheet leases and circular financing among AI firms could make losses worse if returns disappoint. Meta's stock sale is exposed too. If a rate scare knocks its share price down, the sale brings in less money.

Where I land for now: the hike probably won't change how much these companies spend next year. Alphabet and Amazon have both said they'll spend more in 2027. What would actually slow them down is investors losing faith in the cash flows, and I'd watch the order book on the next big bond deal for that before I'd watch the Fed.

What would change my mind

If a big tech bond deal comes in barely covered or gets pulled, or one of these companies cuts its 2027 capex and blames funding costs, I'd be wrong. That would mean investors are starting to doubt the cash flows, and the Fed would matter a lot more.
