Aswath Damodaran's The Corporate Life Cycle came out in August 2024, along with a class he built on the same idea. The core argument fits on an index card, and it's useful enough that I keep coming back to it.

The idea

Businesses move through stages. An idea becomes a start-up, the start-up finds a product and grows fast, growth slows into maturity, and eventually the business declines. Each stage has its own financial signature: young companies burn cash and are valued on stories, mature ones throw off cash and are valued on numbers, and declining ones should be handing money back, not chasing growth.

The payoff is in the mismatches. As I read him, the expensive mistakes come from companies that refuse to act their age: mature firms buying growth through acquisitions, declining firms reinvesting as if they were young. Investors make the mirror-image mistake when they value a young company with a mature-company tool, or an old company with a story.

Why it's useful right now

The AI build-out is pushing some of the most mature companies in the world to behave like young ones again. Meta's free cash flow turned negative after it raised the low end of its 2026 capex range to $135–145 billion. That's a high-growth spending pattern from a company most investors had filed under mature.

Going the other way, Eaton, founded in 1911, just grew sales organically by 19% in a quarter. That's the profile of a much younger business.

Where I'd push back

The framework is at its best describing one company's arc. It's less clear about what happens when an entire industry's capital needs change at once. If every hyperscaler re-ages together, the question isn't whether one of them is acting its age. It's whether the stage itself moved.

My bet: until the capex turns into visible free cash flow, the market will treat these giants like growth companies, with growth-company volatility to match. This week was a preview.

What would change my mind

If hyperscaler free cash flow recovers in 2027 without capex cuts, these are mature companies that could afford a growth project, and the life-cycle label never changed.
