Here's the story you'll hear this weekend: the AI trade cracked. Nvidia's numbers were strong, the stocks fell anyway, so the boom must be over.

I don't buy it. Not because AI stocks can't fall, but because the market is telling a simpler story, and it's written in the bond market.

The number that moved

The 10-year Treasury yield closed at about 5.05% on Thursday. At the end of February it was under 4%. That's more than a full percentage point added to the risk-free rate in nine months.

The Fed is pushing the same way. It raised rates in September, held at 3.75% to 4.00% in October with two officials voting for another hike, and after the October jobs report futures priced roughly an 80% chance of a December increase.

On Thursday the S&P 500 fell for a sixth straight day, its longest losing streak in two and a half years, leaving it about 8% below its October high. The AP listed high interest rates right alongside AI worries as the cause.

Why the AI names fall first

Every stock is a claim on future cash flows, discounted back at some rate. A utility pays you mostly in the next few years. A company priced on what AI might earn in 2032 pays you mostly in the distant future. That's duration, the same idea bond traders use: the further out the cash, the more a change in the discount rate moves today's value.

Run the simplest valuation there is: one dollar of cash flow next year, growing 3% a year forever. At an 8% discount rate it's worth $20. At 9.5% it's worth about $15.38. Nothing about the business changed. The value fell 23%.

And 9.5% isn't a scary hypothetical. Aswath Damodaran's implied equity risk premium was 4.20% on October 1, measured against a 5.29% Treasury rate. Add the two and the cost of equity for the average stock is right around 9.5%.

My take

The AI story didn't break this week. The price of waiting for it went up. That distinction matters, because it changes what you do about it. If the problem were AI demand, you'd sell everything that depends on it. If the problem is the discount rate, you look for businesses whose cash shows up sooner: real orders, real backlog, real free cash flow this year.

That's why I'm more interested in the companies selling power equipment into data centers than in the ones promising what data centers will earn in five years. Same theme, shorter duration.

What would change my mind

If yields fall back below 4.5% and the AI names keep sliding anyway, this was never a rates story. And if analysts start cutting 2027 earnings estimates rather than multiples, the problem is the business, not the discount rate.
