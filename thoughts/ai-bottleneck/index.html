Microsoft just told you where the AI bottleneck is

It isn't chips. When the biggest buyer of AI capacity can't spend money fast enough, the constraint is buildings, power and the gear in between.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 20262 min read

Buried in Microsoft's October earnings call was a line most people skipped. The company trimmed its calendar 2026 capital spending forecast to about $170 billion, down from $175 billion. Not because demand slowed. Because capacity it had contracted from third parties, GPU-as-a-service deals, slipped into the second half of its fiscal year.

The September quarter tells the same story. Microsoft spent $45.2 billion on capex, about $6.5 billion less than analysts expected, and still roughly 30% more than a year earlier.

Think about what that means. The richest buyer in the AI build-out had the money, the demand and the contracts. What it didn't have was finished capacity on time.

Follow the order book, not the headlines

If the bottleneck is physical, the companies selling into it should be swamped. They are. Eaton, which makes switchgear, power distribution gear, UPS systems and now liquid cooling, said its Electrical backlog topped $20 billion, up 46% from a year ago, and data-center sales in its Americas electrical business grew 54% in the quarter.

Across the five biggest spenders, 2026 capex is now forecast at roughly $800 billion or more. Chips take most of it. But every chip needs a building, a grid connection and a power train, and those take longer to build than a GPU takes to ship.

The new bottleneck is political

Here's the twist. Once the constraint is physical, it becomes local. After the midterms, Texas's governor-elect pledged to halt new data-center approvals until new rules are in place, and power-equipment stocks sold off hard.

So my read: AI demand isn't the main risk for the companies building the physical layer. Permits, power and politics are. That's a very different risk to underwrite. It moves slower, and it shows up in orders long before it shows up in revenue.

What would change my mind

If Microsoft's delayed capacity arrives on schedule and lead times on electrical equipment start getting shorter, the bottleneck is easing, and so is the pricing power of the companies that own it.
