Private credit's scariest number isn't the default rate

Fitch's default rate has sat above 6% since April. Half of those defaults never reach a courtroom. The borrowers just stop paying cash.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 20262 min read

Fitch's U.S. private credit default rate was 6.2% for the twelve months through October, barely off September's 6.3%. It has stayed at or above 6% since April, when it first set a record.

That sounds alarming. What's underneath it is more interesting.

6.2%Fitch private credit default rate, 12 months to October
49%Defaults from deferred or payment-in-kind interest
38%Defaults from distressed maturity extensions
0.4%Default rate counting only bankruptcies and restructurings

Almost half of the default events came from interest deferrals or switching to payment-in-kind, where the borrower pays interest with more debt instead of cash. Another 38% were distressed maturity extensions. The rate that counts only bankruptcies, liquidations and restructurings? 0.4%.

Defaults without the drama

In the public bond market, a default is loud: a missed coupon, a price collapse, a court filing. In private credit it's quiet. Lender and borrower renegotiate behind closed doors, the coupon becomes an IOU, and the loan stays on the books.

That's not fraud. It's often the rational move. But it means the losses aren't avoided. They're deferred, and they compound, because payment-in-kind interest grows the very debt the borrower couldn't service in cash.

Why the Fed matters here

Direct loans are almost all floating rate, so every Fed hike lands straight on borrowers' interest bills. The Fed hiked in September and the market expects another move in December. The borrowers already paying in kind now face higher cash costs on bigger balances.

The industry knows it. In PwC's 2026 survey of credit portfolio managers, 64% named defaults and credit losses as a main driver of fund performance this year. PitchBook found non-accrual loans at business development companies growing faster than the BDC market itself. And Johnson Associates expects private credit to be the weakest corner of Wall Street pay this year.

My take

Stop watching the default rate. Watch the payment-in-kind share in BDC filings, and how many of those loans switch back to paying cash. If they do, this was a bridge. If they don't, the 0.4% bankruptcy rate is the number that moves next.

What would change my mind

Rising cure rates on payment-in-kind loans, and recoveries near par on the restructurings that do happen. That would mean lenders were right to be patient.
