How's our trust in AI this week?

The 10-year Treasury yield fell this week, and the S&P 500 dropped 8.6% anyway. Investors are charging more to hold risk, and the AI trade sits in the middle of it.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 22, 2026

When I first sat down to write about this sell-off, I had a tidy explanation. The 10-year Treasury yield went from under 4% in February to above 5% in October. Every stock is a claim on future cash flows, and when the rate you discount those cash flows at rises by more than a full point, the companies whose cash flows sit furthest in the future get hit hardest. The AI names are about as far out on that curve as the market gets, so it seemed natural that they would fall first. I still think that story explains a good part of 2026. It does not explain this week.

Between the close on November 13 and the close on November 20, the S&P 500 fell 8.6%, its worst week since April 2025, and finished Friday at 6,910, more than 10% below its October 6 record. That makes it a correction by the usual definition. The Nasdaq lost 10% and gave back everything it had gained in 2026. Over the same week, the 10-year yield fell from about 5.15% to 4.92%. If the price of money were doing the selling, lower yields should have cushioned stocks. Instead investors sold stocks and bought Treasuries, which is what a flight to safety looks like. In this piece, I want to separate the two parts of the rate we use to value stocks, estimate which one moved, and think through what that means for the AI trade.

Two parts of a discount rate

The rate you use to value a stock, its cost of equity, has two parts. The first is the risk-free rate, what you can earn without taking any risk at all; for dollar investors the 10-year Treasury yield is the usual stand-in. The second is the equity risk premium, the extra return investors demand for owning stocks instead. The first is quoted every second in the bond market. The second is never quoted anywhere. It lives inside stock prices, and you have to back it out.

The most widely used public estimate is Aswath Damodaran's implied equity risk premium, which he updates at the start of each month by asking what return investors must be expecting, given the level of the S&P 500 and the cash flows it is expected to deliver. His last two readings already hint at the story:

In October, the Treasury rate fell 0.35 percentage points and the premium rose 0.21 points, so the two nearly offset and the cost of equity barely moved. Stocks did not have a bad October because money got more expensive. They had one because investors got more nervous at the same time money got a little cheaper.

Backing out this week's risk premium

I can't rerun Damodaran's full model on a weekend, but a simpler version gets close. Think of the market as a growing perpetuity: its value is next year's cash flow divided by the cost of equity minus the growth rate. Damodaran's model assumes that, in the long run, nominal growth can't outrun the risk-free rate, so he sets the two equal. Do that, and the cost of equity minus growth collapses to just the risk premium. The risk-free rate drops out entirely, and the value of the market moves inversely with the premium.

That gives a quick rule. If expected cash flows didn't change, the premium must have risen by about as much as the market fell. From the October 30 close that sits behind Damodaran's November 1 number (7,580) to Friday's close (6,910), the S&P 500 fell 8.8%. Holding cash flows constant, that takes the premium from 4.41% to about 4.84%, and the cost of equity from 9.35% to about 9.76%. Over the same three weeks, the Treasury rate moved two hundredths of a point.

This is a crude estimate, and it leans on the assumption that expected cash flows held still. If analysts are quietly cutting 2027 earnings, part of the drop is about cash flows, and the premium rose by less. But even with generous allowances for that, the direction is clear: the move this month was in the price of risk, not the price of money.

What moved the price of risk

Most of what investors reacted to this week was not news about the cash flows of the AI build-out. Nvidia reported its quarter on November 17 with $115.8 billion of revenue and $2.68 of adjusted earnings per share, both well above estimates, and guided to about $130 billion for the next quarter. The stock still fell 23% over the week, including 14% on Friday to $125.53, after Reuters reported that SEC staff were looking at possible accounting issues, a report neither the SEC nor Nvidia had confirmed. That came eight days after Jensen Huang resigned as CEO. Anthropic priced the largest IPO on record at $83 a share on November 18, opened at $111.50, closed its first day at $97.17, and fell another 8.5% on Friday. And Fed officials spent the week signaling that policy may need to get tighter.

Put those together and you get a market questioning trust, governance and how much to pay for a story. Nothing this week told it the AI build-out will deliver less cash than it expected a month ago. Those are exactly the things investors charge a premium for, and a premium, unlike a single company's bad quarter, gets charged on every stock in the market.

My take

The distinction matters because it changes what you do. A rise in the risk-free rate is mechanical: you can see it, hedge it and work out which assets it hurts most. A rise in the risk premium is a judgment about fear, and fear can reverse quickly or feed on itself. Premiums tend to spike in a scare and come back down when the uncertainty behind them resolves. Some of this week's uncertainty has dates attached: Nvidia will name a permanent CEO, the SEC question will be confirmed or put to rest, and Anthropic will settle into a trading range.

So my read is that the AI story didn't break this week. Investors' trust in it did, and they are now charging more to own it. If you think that fear fades as those questions are answered, a higher premium today is an opportunity. If you think it's the start of a longer repricing, the companies to own are the ones whose cash flows show up soonest, because a higher premium does the least damage to near-term cash. That is the lens I used to revisit my Eaton pitch, and it changed my answer.

What would change my mind

If the premium settles back toward 4.4% and AI stocks keep falling anyway, the market is telling us the cash flows themselves are in doubt, which is a different and worse story. The tell will be 2027 earnings estimates for the AI names: if those start coming down, the problem has moved from the price of risk to the business itself.

Revised November 22, 2026. The first version, written before Friday's close, argued that the sell-off was mostly about a 5% risk-free rate. Friday's numbers didn't fit that story, so I rewrote it.
