Microsoft just told you where the AI bottleneck is

Microsoft is spending as fast as it can and still running short of AI capacity. The holdup is buildings, grid connections and the electrical gear in between.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 2026

There was a line in Microsoft's October earnings call that deserved more attention than it got. The company trimmed its calendar 2026 capital spending forecast to about $170 billion, down from $175 billion, and the reason it gave had nothing to do with demand: capacity it had contracted from third parties, in GPU-as-a-service deals, slipped into the second half of its fiscal year.

The September quarter tells the same story. Microsoft spent $45.2 billion on capex, about $6.5 billion less than analysts expected, and still roughly 30% more than a year earlier.

If you take the company at its word, the richest buyer in the AI build-out had the money, the demand and the contracts, and what held it back was capacity that wasn't finished on time. That is a different constraint from the one most of the market has been arguing about, which is whether demand for AI will hold up.

Follow the order book

If the bottleneck is physical, the companies selling into it should be swamped with orders, and the evidence says they are. Eaton, which makes switchgear, power distribution gear, UPS systems and now liquid cooling, said its Electrical backlog topped $20 billion, up 46% from a year ago, and data-center sales in its Americas electrical business grew 54% in the quarter.

Across the five biggest spenders, 2026 capex is now forecast at roughly $800 billion or more, and chips take most of it. Every chip, though, needs a building, a grid connection and a power train, and those take longer to build than a GPU takes to ship.

A physical bottleneck is also a local one

Once the constraint is physical, it also becomes local, and that brings in decision makers that investors in chip companies rarely had to think about: state governments, utilities and permitting boards. After the midterms, Texas's governor-elect pledged to halt new data-center approvals until new rules are in place, and power-equipment stocks sold off hard.

For the companies building the physical layer, I think the main risk over the next few years is permits, power and politics, more than AI demand. That risk is harder to model, because it comes from regulators and utilities rather than customers. It also moves more slowly, and it shows up in orders long before it shows up in revenue, which gives investors time to see it coming if they watch the order books.

What would change my mind

If Microsoft's delayed capacity arrives on schedule and lead times on electrical equipment start getting shorter, the bottleneck is easing, and so is the pricing power of the companies that own it.
