Notes/Hot take

The talk on the street is about the Fed raising rates, but do the big spenders care?

A quarter point is the smallest number in the AI buildout. The money behind it is priced on what investors expect AI to earn, so the hike lands on stock prices and on the borrowers underneath, not on the spending.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 24, 2026

Wall Street has made up its mind about December. After a hot October inflation report, futures traders put the odds of a quarter-point hike at the Fed's December 8–9 meeting above 90%, and the minutes of the October meeting, released on November 18, showed almost every official expecting more hikes. The textbook says higher rates slow investment, and the biggest investment program in the economy right now belongs to four companies. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta are on track to spend more than $720 billion on data centers and equipment this year, about three times what they spent two years ago. So the question I want to answer is a narrow one: will another hike change how much they spend? I don't think it will, and the reason says a lot about who actually sets the price of money for AI.

A rate has two parts, and the Fed sets only one

When a company decides whether to build something, it compares the return it expects with its cost of capital, the return its investors demand for handing over the money. That cost has two parts. The first is the risk-free rate, what you could earn lending to the US government. The second is a risk premium, the extra return investors want for lending to, or owning a piece of, this particular company. On a bond, the premium shows up as the spread over Treasuries. On a stock, it is the equity risk premium. The Fed sets the overnight rate, and the bond market passes that along to longer rates unevenly. The premium is set by investors, and it moves with what they believe about the borrower's future cash flows.

That is why a quarter point does little to a project investors love. If you believe a data center will earn 15% a year, raising its cost of capital from 9% to 9.25% will not change your mind. If you believe it will earn 7%, no rate cut would rescue it. The decision turns on the expected return, and the Fed has no say in that.

The next dollar comes from people betting on AI

Until recently, the big spenders paid for their buildouts out of their own cash flow. Microsoft still can, with free cash flow of $18.6 billion last quarter. The others now spend more than their businesses bring in. Amazon's free cash flow over the twelve months to September was negative $42.4 billion, and Alphabet and Meta were both negative in the September quarter. The gap is being filled with outside money, and each source of it is a bet on future AI cash flows. The hyperscalers sold more than $370.7 billion of bonds this year through November 4, about a fifth of all US investment-grade issuance in a record year. Meta has also agreed to forward sales of 46 million shares, worth about $28.7 billion at the initial price, which it can settle any time before November 2027. Underneath that sits private credit. Blue Owl and Pimco helped Meta raise $27.3 billion of bonds last year for its Louisiana campus through a joint venture that keeps the debt off Meta's balance sheet, and data center developers have raised more than $40 billion through similar private placements.

Even on those generous assumptions, the hike costs these companies under $1 billion a year. That is about 0.13% of this year's capex, and less than 3% of the cash Meta's operations produced in a single quarter. It is hard to picture a board cancelling a data center over it.

Investors already charge more, and the money keeps coming

The stronger evidence is that the price of this money has already risen by far more than a quarter point, and investors kept supplying it. In October 2025, Meta sold a bond due in 2055 with a 5.625% coupon. On November 5 this year it sold one due in 2056 at 7.50%, nearly two points higher, or seven and a half Fed hikes' worth. Orders still reached about $58 billion for a $15 billion deal, and a week later Meta's first euro bond drew a record €53 billion of orders for €9 billion. Further down the credit ladder the premiums are larger. A credit default swap is insurance against a company not paying its debts, and its yearly cost is the market's read on that risk. In October, insuring Oracle's debt for five years cost about 3 points a year, a level an S&P Global analyst compared with junk-rated companies. For CoreWeave, which rents out AI computing and carries roughly $50 billion of debt, it cost about 10.

Set out this way, the hike is the smallest number in the table. The investors funding the buildout are pricing the risk that AI cash flows disappoint, and they charge for that risk in whole percentage points. A move in the risk-free rate a quarter that size does not decide whether the money shows up. What decides it is whether those investors keep believing in the cash flows. That is also what the Fed itself is watching: almost every official at the October meeting discussed what would happen if returns on AI investment disappointed.

Where the hike does land

Rates still matter here, but more to the price of these companies than to their spending. Most of a data center's payoff comes years out, and distant cash flows are the ones most sensitive to the discount rate. In my own illustration with a 9% cost of capital, adding a quarter point cuts the present value of a dollar due next year by 0.2%, while a dollar due in twenty years loses 4.5%. The market has already done most of that work. The 10-year Treasury yield has risen about a full point over the past year, to 5.05% on November 19, so a December hike adds only a little to a valuation hit that has largely happened.

The second place is underneath the hyperscalers. When Meta or Microsoft signs a long lease on a data center, the developer that built it often borrowed to do so, and much of private credit is floating-rate, so a Fed hike reaches those borrowers right away. Fed staff warned in October that off-balance-sheet leases and circular financing among AI firms could amplify losses if returns disappoint. There is an equity channel too: Meta's forward sale raises less money if its share price falls, so a rate-driven sell-off would shrink one of its sources of funding.

My take

I expect Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta to keep raising their spending in 2027, hike or no hike. Alphabet's finance chief has said capex will rise significantly next year, and Amazon's CEO expects to spend meaningfully more. For an investor in these stocks, the hike shows up in the multiple you pay for cash flows a decade away, not in the size of the buildout. For a lender to the companies below them, it shows up as interest. If you want to know whether money for AI is getting harder to raise, the order book on the next jumbo bond will tell you more than the Fed's projections will.

What would change my mind

If a big bond deal from one of these companies comes in barely covered or gets pulled, or Meta delays settling its forward share sale, I would read that as investors losing faith in the cash flows, and that would slow the spending far more than the Fed can. A hyperscaler cutting its 2027 capex and blaming funding costs would also prove me wrong. So would Oracle losing its investment-grade rating, because funds that may hold only investment-grade debt would have to sell.
