Almost half of private credit's defaults never reach a courtroom

Fitch's default rate has been at or above 6% since April. Many of those borrowers now pay interest with more debt instead of cash, which delays the losses without avoiding them.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 2026

Fitch's U.S. private credit default rate was 6.2% for the twelve months through October, barely off September's 6.3%. It has stayed at or above 6% since April, when it first set a record.

That is high enough to worry about, but the headline hides most of what matters, because of what gets counted as a default.

6.2%Fitch private credit default rate, 12 months to October
49%Defaults from deferred or payment-in-kind interest
38%Defaults from distressed maturity extensions
0.4%Default rate counting only bankruptcies and restructurings

Almost half of the default events came from interest deferrals or switching to payment-in-kind, where the borrower pays interest with more debt instead of cash. Another 38% were distressed maturity extensions. Count only bankruptcies, liquidations and restructurings, and the default rate is 0.4%.

Why private credit defaults are quiet

In the public bond market, a default is hard to miss: a coupon goes unpaid, the bond's price collapses and, often, someone files in court. In private credit, the lender and the borrower usually renegotiate privately, the cash coupon becomes an IOU, and the loan stays on the books.

There is nothing improper about this, and for a lender who believes the business will recover, it is often the sensible choice. The cost is that the losses are deferred, and they compound in the meantime, because payment-in-kind interest adds to the very debt the borrower could not service in cash.

Why the Fed matters here

Direct loans are almost all floating rate, so every Fed hike lands straight on borrowers' interest bills. The Fed hiked in September and the market expects another move in December. The borrowers already paying in kind now face higher cash costs on bigger balances.

The people running these funds see it too. In PwC's 2026 survey of credit portfolio managers, 64% named defaults and credit losses as a main driver of fund performance this year. PitchBook found non-accrual loans at business development companies growing faster than the BDC market itself. And Johnson Associates expects private credit to be the weakest corner of Wall Street pay this year.

My take

I would spend less time on the headline default rate and more on two numbers in business development company filings: the share of loans paying in kind, and how many of those switch back to paying cash. If most of them do, payment-in-kind was a bridge that carried borrowers through a stretch of high rates. If they don't, I expect the 0.4% bankruptcy rate to be the number that moves next.

What would change my mind

Rising cure rates on payment-in-kind loans, and recoveries near par on the restructurings that do happen. That would mean lenders were right to be patient.
