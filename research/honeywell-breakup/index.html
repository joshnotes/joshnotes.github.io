Honeywell, in three pieces: what the breakup did and what to watch

The separation is done. The verdict comes with the first full year of standalone numbers for Honeywell Technologies, Honeywell Aerospace and Solstice.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 2026

3Independent companies from one
Oct 30, 2025Solstice Advanced Materials spun off
Jun 29, 2026Honeywell Aerospace spun off
HON, HONA, SOLSTickers today

Honeywell's breakup is finished. What began as a plan announced in October 2024 to spin off its Advanced Materials business ended on June 29, 2026, when Honeywell Aerospace started trading on its own. The company left behind, renamed Honeywell Technologies, is now a pure-play automation business across buildings, process and industrial markets.

What happened, step by step

  • October 2024. Honeywell announced plans to spin off Advanced Materials.
  • October 30, 2025. Solstice Advanced Materials began trading as SOLS. Shareholders received one Solstice share for every four Honeywell shares. Solstice had about $3.8 billion of net sales in 2024 and raised $1 billion of senior notes ahead of the spin.
  • June 29, 2026. Honeywell Aerospace began trading as HONA. Shareholders received one Aerospace share for every two Honeywell shares, and Honeywell Technologies completed a 1-for-2 reverse stock split. Honeywell Technologies kept the HON ticker.

Why companies split up

The case for a breakup rests on focus. A conglomerate makes its businesses compete for the same pool of capital and management attention, and investors who want one of them have to buy all of them. Standalone companies can set their own capital allocation, pay their managers on their own results, and attract shareholders who want that specific exposure. Honeywell's management framed it the same way: each company now has a distinct strategy and its own financial flexibility.

What it costs

Breakups also cost money. Each new company needs its own board, finance team and systems, and shared costs that used to be spread across three businesses have to be absorbed or cut. Debt has to be divided too, which is why Solstice raised its own notes before separating. These dis-synergies are real, and most of them land in the first year or two.

How a spin-off works

The parent distributes shares of the new company to its own shareholders, usually tax-free, based on who owns the parent on a record date. Before the distribution, the parent's stock can trade two ways: with the right to receive the new shares, or "ex-distribution" without it. Honeywell's ex-distribution shares traded under a temporary ticker, HONIV, before the Solstice spin. Shareholders entitled to fractional shares receive cash instead.

How to judge whether it worked

It is too early to tell. The test is whether the three companies together end up worth more than Honeywell would have been, after the extra costs. That needs a full year of standalone results, and the first full-year reports arrive in early 2027. Until then, the useful checks are:

  • Whether Honeywell Technologies delivers against the three-year targets it set at its June 11, 2026 investor day.
  • Whether standalone margins at HONA and SOLS hold up once separation costs are behind them.
  • Whether the pure-plays use their focus well, through reinvestment, buybacks, or by becoming acquirers or targets.

View

I am neutral until the numbers come in. The structure makes sense on paper, and the first full year of standalone results, in early 2027, will show whether it pays for its costs.
