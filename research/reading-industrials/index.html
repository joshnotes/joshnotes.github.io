How to read a diversified industrial in eight numbers

Orders, backlog, organic growth, margins and cash. A field guide to the sector, worked through with Eaton's latest quarter.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 2026

Diversified industrials are hard to read from the income statement alone. They sell into dozens of end markets, take orders months or years before they ship, and buy and sell businesses constantly. Here are the eight numbers that do most of the work, using Eaton's third quarter of 2026 as the worked example.

1. Organic growth

Strip out acquisitions and currency to see what the existing business did. Eaton's sales rose 28% in the quarter, but only 19 points were organic. Eight came from acquisitions and one from currency. The organic number is the one that tells you about demand.

2. Orders, on a rolling 12-month basis

Orders are the leading indicator. Big projects make single quarters lumpy, so industrials often report a rolling 12-month average. Eaton's rose 39% organically in Electrical Americas and 51% in Electrical Global.

3. Backlog

Orders not yet shipped. Eaton's Electrical backlog topped $20 billion, up 46%. Rising backlog means revenue visibility, but also execution risk if capacity can't keep up.

4. Book-to-bill

Orders divided by sales. Above 1, backlog is growing; below 1, the business is eating into it. It's the simplest early warning sign in the sector.

5. Segment margin

Profit at the business-unit level, before corporate costs. Eaton's segment margin hit a record 25.3%, with Electrical Americas at 29.8%. Comparing segments tells you where the value in the company sits.

6. Incremental margin

The change in profit divided by the change in sales. It shows how much of each new dollar of revenue drops to the bottom line, and whether pricing and volume are outrunning costs. Strong incrementals are why industrials re-rate in upcycles.

7. Free cash flow

Earnings are an opinion; cash is a fact. Eaton generated $1.31 billion of operating cash flow and $1.03 billion of free cash flow after $275 million of capex. Free cash flow conversion, free cash flow divided by net income, shows whether earnings turn into cash or get stuck in inventory and receivables.

8. Adjusted versus GAAP earnings

Acquisitive companies report a lot of adjustments, so check what's excluded. Eaton's adjusted EPS was $3.68 against GAAP EPS of $2.77. Most of the gap was $0.62 of intangible amortization and $0.30 of acquisition and divestiture costs. Amortization is a reasonable exclusion; recurring deal costs at a serial acquirer deserve more skepticism.

Two more things to know

  • Short cycle versus long cycle. Short-cycle products ship in weeks and react to the economy quickly. Long-cycle projects, like grid equipment and aerospace, are booked far ahead and turn slowly in both directions.
  • Portfolio moves. Industrials constantly reshape themselves through acquisitions and breakups. Honeywell's three-way split is the latest example, and it's why organic growth and segment data matter more than headline totals.
