Industrials 101: eight numbers that tell the real story

The eight numbers I use to read an industrial company, from orders and backlog to margins and cash, worked through with Eaton's latest quarter.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 21, 2026

Diversified industrials are hard to read from the income statement alone. They sell into dozens of end markets, take orders months or years before they ship, and buy and sell businesses constantly. I find that eight numbers do most of the work, and I'll use Eaton's third quarter of 2026 to show each one.

1. Organic growth

Organic growth strips out acquisitions and currency to show what the existing business did. Eaton's sales rose 28% in the quarter, but only 19 points of that were organic, with eight coming from acquisitions and one from currency. The organic number is the one that tells you about demand, and it is the first one I look for.

2. Orders, on a rolling 12-month basis

Orders lead revenue, often by many months, which makes them the best early read on where a business is heading. Because a few big projects can make any single quarter lumpy, industrials often report orders on a rolling 12-month basis. Eaton's rose 39% organically in Electrical Americas and 51% in Electrical Global.

3. Backlog

Backlog is the stock of orders that have been booked but not yet shipped. Eaton's Electrical backlog topped $20 billion, up 46%. A growing backlog gives you visibility into future revenue, but it also carries execution risk, because the company still has to build what it has sold, and its capacity may not keep up.

4. Book-to-bill

Book-to-bill is orders divided by sales. Above 1, the backlog is growing; below 1, the business is shipping more than it books and eating into what it has. Because it turns before revenue does, it is the simplest early warning sign in the sector.

5. Segment margin

Segment margin is profit at the business-unit level, before corporate costs. Eaton's hit a record 25.3%, with Electrical Americas at 29.8%. Comparing margins across segments shows where the value in a company sits, which at Eaton is Electrical Americas, the segment that earns more than half of its segment profit.

6. Incremental margin

Incremental margin is the change in profit divided by the change in sales, which tells you how much of each new dollar of revenue reaches profit. When it runs well above the average margin, pricing and volume are outrunning costs, and that is usually what gets industrial stocks re-rated in an upcycle.

7. Free cash flow

Earnings depend on accounting choices; cash flow depends on far fewer of them. Eaton generated $1.31 billion of operating cash flow and $1.03 billion of free cash flow after $275 million of capex. Free cash flow conversion, free cash flow divided by net income, shows whether earnings turn into cash or get stuck in inventory and receivables, which is a real risk for a company growing as fast as Eaton is.

8. Adjusted versus GAAP earnings

Acquisitive companies report a lot of adjustments, so it is worth checking what's excluded. Eaton's adjusted EPS was $3.68 against GAAP EPS of $2.77. Most of the gap was $0.62 of intangible amortization and $0.30 of acquisition and divestiture costs. Amortization is a reasonable exclusion; recurring deal costs at a serial acquirer deserve more skepticism.

Two more things to know

  • Short cycle versus long cycle. Short-cycle products ship in weeks and react to the economy quickly. Long-cycle projects, like grid equipment and aerospace, are booked far ahead and turn slowly in both directions.
  • Portfolio moves. Industrials constantly reshape themselves through acquisitions and breakups. Honeywell's three-way split is the latest example, and it's why organic growth and segment data matter more than headline totals.
