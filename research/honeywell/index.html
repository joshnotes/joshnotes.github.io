Honeywell after the breakup: a good plan, priced as if it's already done

Honeywell is now a pure automation company growing 4% to 6% a year. Even on management's own targets I value it at about $171 a share, 20% below the price. The market is discounting it like a safer business than it is.

For most of my life, Honeywell was the textbook conglomerate: thermostats, jet engines, refrigerants, warehouse robots and quantum computers under one ticker. That company no longer exists. Solstice Advanced Materials was spun off in October 2025, Honeywell Aerospace in June 2026, and the warehouse and productivity businesses were sold this year. What is left, Honeywell Technologies, sells automation for buildings, process plants and factories, and it just reported its first quarter as a standalone company. I wrote about how the breakup was done separately. This piece asks the harder question: now that the pieces are apart, is the one still called Honeywell worth what the market is paying for it? I work through it in the five steps I use for every company.

1. The industry: three businesses, three sets of rivals

"Automation" sounds like one market, but Honeywell Technologies competes in three, each with its own customers, cycle and competitors.

The common thread is an installed base. Once a refinery runs on your control system or a hospital on your fire panels, you sell it service, software and upgrades for decades, which is why these businesses earn high margins and why investors pay automation multiples for them. The catch is that the cycles differ. Building Automation follows construction and retrofits; Process Automation follows energy capital spending; Industrial Automation follows factory output. A company that owns all three is steadier than any one of them, but it is not a growth company.

2. The business: where Honeywell earns its money

Third-quarter sales were $5.0 billion, up 1% as reported and 7% organically, a measure that strips out acquisitions, divestitures and currency. Segment margin, Honeywell's preferred profit measure, was 21.3%. Profit is concentrated in buildings:

Exhibit 2: Building Automation earns almost half of segment profit, at the highest margin

Segment profit, third quarter of 2026. Segment margins: Building Automation 27.0%, Process Automation and Technology 22.2%, Industrial Automation 18.4%. Corporate and other costs were $101 million. Source: Honeywell Technologies third quarter 2026 results.

Building Automation and Process Automation each grew 8% organically, the latter helped by 14% growth in project sales for LNG and petrochemical plants. Industrial Automation grew 4%. Backlog was close to $19 billion, up 7%. These are good numbers for an industrial company, and management raised its full-year guidance on the back of them. Two other assets sit outside the segments. The first is a 47% stake in Quantinuum, the quantum computing company that went public in June, and Honeywell's equity investments, almost all of them that stake, are carried at $7.3 billion on its balance sheet. At Friday's price, that stake is worth about $3.5 billion, or $11 a Honeywell share. The second is a pension surplus: at the end of 2024 the old Honeywell's plans held $5.7 billion more than they owed, and Honeywell stopped counting the income from that surplus in its adjusted earnings this year.

3. The model: management's targets as my base case

At its June investor day, management set three-year targets: 4% to 6% organic growth, segment margins rising by more than 60 basis points a year to about 24% in 2029, and adjusted earnings per share of about $12 in 2029, with more than 90% of earnings converted into free cash flow. Rather than second-guess those targets, I use them as my base case, which is generous: most companies miss some of their investor-day promises.

One adjustment matters for the cash flows. Segment margin leaves out real costs: corporate overhead, stock-based pay, pension service costs and restructuring, which Honeywell spends money on every year. In the third quarter, those items plus amortization took a 21.3% segment margin down to a 14.8% operating margin. For the cash flow valuation, I use an operating margin after those costs of 17% in 2026, rising to 20.5% by 2029, about 3.5 points below the segment margin target.

4. Valuing it three ways

The discounted cash flow valuation. I value the automation business on its own, then add the Quantinuum stake at market value and half the old pension surplus after tax, on the assumption that roughly half stayed with Honeywell Technologies when Aerospace left.

The input that decides this valuation is the beta. Honeywell's historical beta, measured against the market, is 0.79, which would put its cost of capital near 7.6% and its value near $208, close to the price. But that beta was earned by the old conglomerate, with aerospace and a long record of steady earnings. A business that sells into refinery projects, factory output and commercial construction is more exposed to the economic cycle, not less, so I use 1.0, the beta of the average stock. That one choice is worth about $35 a share, and it is the real disagreement between my number and the market's.

Trading comparables. The automation peers trade at 22.6 to 25.6 times next year's earnings: Schneider Electric at about 22.6, Rockwell Automation at about 24.9 and ABB at about 25.6. Applied to my 2027 estimate of $9.27, plus the Quantinuum stake, that gives $220 to $248. Honeywell already trades at about 22 times 2027 earnings once you take out the stake, so on this measure it looks fairly priced.

Precedent transactions. Honeywell's own recent purchases set useful markers: it paid about 13 times expected EBITDA for Carrier's Global Access Solutions business and about 11 times for Johnson Matthey's Catalyst Technologies, both counting tax benefits and cost savings. Using 2027 segment profit of about $4.5 billion as a rough stand-in for EBITDA, those multiples give $134 to $162. Buyers of whole businesses usually pay more than the stock market does, not less, so the fact that the stock trades well above this range says the market is paying an automation premium on top of a full price.

The pricing approaches and the value approach point in different directions, and I think I know why. The market is pricing Honeywell as an automation company, next to Rockwell and Schneider, and those companies trade at multiples set in a market that was comfortable paying for growth. But Honeywell's own targets describe a mid-single-digit grower with expanding margins, and when you value that plan on its cash flows at a normal cost of capital, it doesn't support $212.

5. The risks, as bear, base and bull cases

For a Sell call, the risks that matter most are the ones that would prove me wrong. Honeywell has $5.7 billion of cash and could buy back a lot of stock. LNG and data center construction could keep organic growth near the 7% of the last quarter rather than the 5% I assume. Quantinuum could re-rate, which would lift the value of the stake. And a cleaner, simpler Honeywell is easier to buy, so a takeover premium is not out of the question. On the downside, process and industrial demand are cyclical, the integration of Johnson Matthey's catalyst business is diluting margins in the near term, and a stake carried at $7.3 billion but worth $3.5 billion may eventually have to be written down.

My take

The breakup did what it was supposed to do. Honeywell Technologies is simpler, its margins are rising and its first standalone quarter beat its own targets. None of that is in question. What I question is the price, which already assumes the plan works and then discounts it as if Honeywell were a lower-risk business than a cyclical automation company really is. My rating is Sell, with a probability-weighted value of about $171. Under my rating rule, it would move to Hold below about $200 and to Buy below about $150. I would also revisit the call sooner if the company shows it can grow organically at 6% or more for several quarters while getting to its margin target early, because that is the bull case, and the bull case is what the price requires.

What would change the view

Fourth-quarter results in early 2027 with organic growth above 6% and segment margins on a clear path past 24%, a large buyback funded from the $5.7 billion of cash, or a monetization of the Quantinuum stake well above its market value. Any of these would move my base case toward the bull case.
