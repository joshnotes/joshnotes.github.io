Eaton got eaten. Is it cheap yet?

The stock is 35% below its high, even after a record quarter and a third guidance raise. I value it at about $281 a share, 10% below the price. Hold, turning to Buy below about $245.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 22, 2026

HoldRating
$281Value per share, probability-weighted
−10%Value vs price
$311.33Price at Nov 20, 2026 close

Eaton is one of the cleanest ways to own the electrical side of the AI build-out, and for most of the past two years the market treated it that way. On November 3, the company reported a record third quarter and raised its full-year guidance for the third time. The stock fell almost 10% the next day, kept falling, and closed at $311.33 on November 20, a new 52-week low and 35% below its high of $478. When a good company's stock falls that far, the temptation is to buy it because it is cheaper than it was. But "cheaper than it was" is a statement about price. Whether it is cheap is a question about value, and the only way I know to answer it is to work through the business from the industry down. That is what I do here, in the same five steps I use for every company.

1. The industry: who sells the power chain

Getting electricity from the grid to a rack of servers takes a long chain of equipment: transformers and switchgear where power enters a site, breakers and busway to move it around, uninterruptible power supplies to keep it clean and constant, and, increasingly, liquid cooling to carry away the heat the chips produce. A handful of large companies sell most of that chain, and Eaton is one of the biggest.

Revenue is not market share, and I would not read too much into the ranking. The more useful point is where the growth is. One market estimate puts data center power infrastructure at about $21.7 billion in 2025, rising to $87.1 billion by 2035, which works out to roughly 15% a year. I treat market research forecasts with suspicion, but the direction matches what the companies themselves are reporting in orders. Two features of this industry matter for valuation. Lead times are long, because the equipment is engineered and the factories are full, so orders show up in revenue quarters or years later. And while capacity is tight, the sellers have pricing power. The risk, as in every capital goods boom, is the moment capacity catches up with demand.

2. The business: what Eaton sells and where it earns

Eaton reports four segments. Electrical Americas and Electrical Global sell the power chain described above. Aerospace sells fuel, hydraulic and power systems for aircraft. Mobility sells transmissions and other parts for trucks and cars, and is on its way out, which I come back to in the model. The profit is concentrated in one place:

Exhibit 2: Electrical Americas earns more than half of Eaton's segment profit

Eaton has also been buying its way further along the chain. It paid $1.43 billion for Fibrebond, a maker of prefabricated power enclosures, in April 2025; $1.53 billion for Ultra PCS, an aerospace systems business, in January 2026; and $9.55 billion for Boyd Thermal, a liquid cooling specialist, in March 2026. It has agreed to buy COL Group, an Italian maker of medium-voltage equipment, for €810 million. Those deals are a big part of why total debt reached $20.1 billion at the end of September.

The reason the market loved this company is in the order book. In this business, orders today are revenue tomorrow, so the most important numbers are not in the income statement:

Exhibit 3: Orders and backlog are growing faster than sales in every segment that reports them

A book-to-bill of 1.2 means Eaton is taking in $1.20 of new orders for every dollar it ships. As long as that holds, revenue growth has somewhere to come from for at least the next year.

3. The model: from guidance to 2027

Management's 2026 guidance is for 13% to 15% organic growth, segment margins of 24.1% to 24.5%, and adjusted earnings per share of $13.70 to $13.80. The complication is Mobility. In June, Eaton agreed to combine the segment with Dana in a Reverse Morris Trust, a tax-efficient way of spinning a business off and merging it with another company in one step. Eaton shareholders will own 50.1% of the combined Dana, Eaton will receive about $1.1 billion in cash, and the deal is expected to close in the first four months of 2027. No value is lost in that trade, since it moves into Dana shares that Eaton shareholders will own directly, but the earnings leave Eaton. By my estimate, Mobility contributes about $1.00 of Eaton's 2026 adjusted earnings per share, and any 2027 forecast that leaves it in is overstating what the remaining company will earn.

With Mobility left in, my 2027 number would be about $15.70, close to the $15.78 Zacks published this week. The difference is almost entirely the spin-off. That matters for the next step, because a 25 times multiple on the wrong earnings number is a $25 mistake per share before you have made a single judgment about the business.

4. Valuing it three ways

I start with intrinsic value, because it is the only one of the three approaches that asks what the business is worth on its own cash flows, and then use the two pricing approaches to see how the market values similar businesses.

The discounted cash flow valuation. I value the company without Mobility and add back what Eaton shareholders will own of the new Dana, using the $5.1 billion deal value less the $1.1 billion Eaton keeps in cash, or about $10 a share. The inputs:

Exhibit 5: At a 9.2% cost of capital, my base case is worth $288 a share

InputAssumption
Revenue growth12% in 2027, slowing by a point a year to 8% in 2031, then down to 3.5% by 2036
Operating margin23.4% in 2027, after corporate costs, rising to 25% by 2031
Reinvestment$1 of capital for every $2.50 of new revenue; 15% return on capital in the long run
Cost of capital9.2%: a 9.95% cost of equity (4.92% Treasury plus a 1.14 beta times a 4.41% risk premium), a 4.6% after-tax cost of debt, and 14% debt
Value per share$288: enterprise value of $127.4 billion, less $19.1 billion of net debt, plus $4.0 billion for the Dana stake, over 389.6 million shares

Net debt is as of September 30, adjusted for the pending COL Group purchase and the $1.1 billion Dana distribution. At an 8.5% cost of capital the value is $334; at 9.0%, $299; at 9.5%, $271; at 10.0%, $247. Estimates are mine. Sources: Eaton filings; Damodaran Online; U.S. Treasury.

The most useful way to read a DCF is backwards. At $311, the market is pricing Eaton as if revenue grows about 11% a year for five years at a 25% operating margin. My base case averages 10%. So the market is not being unreasonable; it is simply paying for a bit more than my base case, and it is doing so at a time when the price of risk is rising. The swing factor is the cost of capital: at the 8.5% that would have looked normal a year ago, this stock is worth $334, and the case for buying it would be easy.

Trading comparables. The closest listed peers traded at 21 to 26 times next year's earnings in October and early November: Vertiv at about 21 times, Schneider Electric at about 23 and ABB at about 26. I leave out GE Vernova, at about 34 times, because most of its business is power generation. Applied to my 2027 estimate, plus the Dana stake, that range gives $318 to $391.

Precedent transactions. Recent deals for power and cooling businesses were struck at 12.7 times expected EBITDA (Eaton's purchase of Fibrebond), about 13 times (Vertiv's purchase of UtilityInnovation Group) and 22.5 times (Eaton's purchase of Boyd Thermal). Eaton doesn't report EBITDA; I put 2027 at about $8.7 billion, close to my estimate of segment operating profit, since depreciation roughly offsets unallocated corporate costs. That gives $245 to $463. These were purchases of smaller, faster-growing businesses, with a premium for control, so I treat the top of this range as a ceiling, not a target.

The two pricing approaches say Eaton is cheap relative to its peers and to its own recent history. The DCF says it is roughly fairly priced, perhaps a little rich. I side with the DCF here, for a simple reason: the peer multiples were set in the same market that just repriced risk, and a stock that is cheap relative to expensive peers can still be expensive.

5. The risks, as bear, base and bull cases

The method says to write the risks first, so here they are as scenarios with values attached rather than a list of worries. The main risks are local pauses on new data center permits, which could show up in 2027 orders; hyperscalers cutting capex if their own free cash flow comes under pressure; a higher risk premium for longer; the integration of more than $11 billion of acquisitions while adding factory capacity quickly; and higher commodity costs, which management blamed for most of the year-over-year margin decline in Electrical Americas.

Dollars per share. Dashed line: the November 20 close, $311.33. Bear: data center orders roll over in 2027, revenue grows 4% and then 2%, and operating margins slip to 21.5%. Base: as in Exhibit 5. Bull: the backlog converts faster, revenue grows 16% and then 15%, and margins reach 26%. Estimates are mine.

I give the bear case only 20% because a 1.2 book-to-bill and a backlog that is growing faster than sales make a sudden collapse less likely than a slowdown. Even so, the probability-weighted value of about $281 sits 10% below the price. The bull case is worth 14% more than the price; the bear case is worth 46% less. That asymmetry is the whole argument.

My take

Eaton is a great business with a full price. The 35% fall has taken the stock from expensive to roughly fair. I would not sell it if I owned it, because the order book gives the bull case real support, but I would not buy it at $311 either. My rating is Hold. Under the rating rule I use for every pitch, it would become a Buy below about $245, where my value is 15% above the price, and a Sell above about $330.

What would change the view

Upward: fourth-quarter results, expected around February 2, 2027, with book-to-bill still at 1.2 or higher and margins at or above the 25.4% to 25.8% guidance would make me raise my growth assumptions. Downward: rolling 12-month orders in Electrical Americas turning negative, or book-to-bill falling below 1, would mean the slowdown has started.
