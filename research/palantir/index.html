Palantir: growing 96% a year, and priced for even more

Palantir is growing 96% a year at margins few software companies ever reach. I still value it at about $75 a share, half the price, because the price needs about $106 billion of revenue by 2036.

Joshuva JohnsonNov 22, 2026

Palantir Technologies (PLTR) just reported the kind of quarter that most software companies never have. Revenue grew 96% from a year earlier to $2.31 billion, its commercial business in the United States passed its government business for the first time, and it kept 62 cents of every dollar of revenue as adjusted operating income. The stock has gone the other way. It closed at $143.49 on November 20, a third below its 52-week high of $212.94, after falling with the rest of the AI trade. When a business keeps getting better while its stock falls, the obvious question is whether the market has finally given investors a reasonable price. I work through that question in the five steps I use for every company.

1. The industry: software that turns data into decisions

Palantir sells platforms that pull an organization's data together, model how its operations fit together, and let people and AI models act on that picture. The closest rivals are the data and AI platforms that large companies are standardizing on: Databricks and Snowflake on the data side, ServiceNow on workflows, and the cloud giants, who sell overlapping tools inside their own platforms. Every one of these companies is growing, because AI projects need organized data before they need anything else. Palantir is growing the fastest, and it is already the second largest of the four.

2. The business: where Palantir earns its money

Palantir reports two customer groups, governments and companies, and splits each between the United States and the rest of the world. The U.S. is now 84% of revenue, and the growth is coming from American companies: U.S. commercial revenue grew 148% in the third quarter, with 64 deals worth $10 million or more closed in the quarter. Government revenue is still growing at a pace any other contractor would envy, 96% in the U.S., and no single customer accounts for more than 10% of revenue.

The profitability is as unusual as the growth. In the third quarter, Palantir earned $1.08 billion of operating income on a GAAP basis, a 47% margin, and $1.43 billion on its adjusted basis, a 62% margin. The difference between the two is mostly stock-based pay, $319 million in the quarter, or about 14% of revenue. I treat stock pay as an expense, the same way I would treat a salary, so every margin in my valuation is a GAAP margin, about 15 points below the adjusted margins the company headlines. Palantir also holds $10.6 billion of cash and marketable securities, $10.4 billion of it in cash and U.S. Treasuries, and has no debt.

3. The model: guidance for 2026, the analysts for 2027

Palantir has raised its 2026 revenue guidance three times this year, from about $7.19 billion in February to $8.53 billion now, which would be growth of 91%. Its fourth-quarter guidance, $2.65 billion at the midpoint, implies growth of about 88% from a year earlier, a little slower than the third quarter's 96% but still extraordinary. For 2027, analysts expected revenue of about $12.2 billion to $12.3 billion before the third-quarter report, growth of roughly 50%. I use 55% for my base case, a little above that, to reflect the raise since.

4. Valuing it three ways

The discounted cash flow valuation. I value Palantir on ten years of cash flows, letting growth slow each year from 55% in 2027 to 3.5% by 2036 and letting the margin ease from 47% to 45% as the business matures and competitors catch up. I use the diluted share count, 2.57 billion shares, which counts the 147 million options employees hold, most of them deep in the money at an average exercise price of about $10.

The number that decides this valuation is growth, far more than the discount rate. Moving the cost of capital by a full point changes the value by about $4 a share. To get to the market price instead, I have to assume revenue of about $106 billion by 2036, growing 29% a year for a decade, with a 50% operating margin. That is more than three and a half times what ServiceNow, Databricks and Snowflake together bring in this year. It could happen, and Palantir has beaten most of the forecasts made for it so far. But a price that requires it leaves no room for the ordinary disappointments that every fast-growing company eventually has.

Trading comparables. The software companies the market pays the most for traded in August at about 34 times next year's revenue for CrowdStrike, 33 times for Cloudflare and 18 times for Snowflake. Applied to my 2027 revenue estimate, that range gives $94 to $181 a share. Palantir trades at about 27 times that estimate, inside the range, while growing much faster than any of these companies, so on this measure it looks reasonable. I give this approach less weight than usual, because every company in it is also priced on how long its growth lasts, which is the very question at issue.

Precedent transactions. Buyers paid high multiples for AI data businesses this year: Schneider Electric paid 18.2 times revenue for Cognite, an industrial data platform for energy and manufacturing companies, a market Palantir also sells into, and Autodesk paid 26.7 times recurring revenue for MaintainX. Applied to Palantir's 2026 revenue, those multiples give $64 to $92 a share. No acquirer could write a check for a company worth about $370 billion, so I read this range as what buyers pay for AI data assets today, which is useful context but a weak guide to a takeover price.

The pricing approaches and the value approaches point in different directions here, and the gap is wider than for any company I have pitched. The market is pricing Palantir as the best company in a group of expensive growth stocks, and on that comparison the price is defensible. A cash flow valuation asks a harder question, which is how much cash the business will produce over its life, and the answer only reaches today's price if growth stays exceptional for a decade.

5. The risks, as bear, base and bull cases

The first risk is already visible in the bookings. Total contract value signed in the third quarter grew just 6% from a year earlier, and U.S. commercial contracts 11%, far slower than revenue. The value of U.S. commercial deals still to be delivered grew 4% from the second quarter, after 27% the quarter before. Shorter contracts may explain some of that, and analysts disagree about what it means, but revenue cannot grow at 96% for long if signings grow at 6%. The second is concentration in government work, still 41% of revenue in the U.S. alone and exposed to budgets and politics. The third is dilution: stock pay runs at about 14% of revenue, insiders have been steady sellers, and the chief executive filed after the close on November 20 to sell about $100 million of stock under a trading plan set in March. The founders also control just under half of the votes through a special share class. The fourth is competition from Databricks, which is growing more than 80% a year, and from the cloud giants. On the other side, Palantir has beaten analysts' revenue estimates quarter after quarter, and if U.S. commercial growth stays above 100% through 2027, my base case will look too cautious.

My take

Palantir is an exceptional business, and the third quarter made the case stronger: the growth is broadening from governments to companies, and the margins are rising with it. What I can't get to is the price. Even my bull case, which has Palantir taking in about $87 billion a year by 2036, is worth less than the stock costs today. My rating is Sell, with a probability-weighted value of about $75 a share. Under my rating rule, it would move to Hold below about $88 and to Buy below about $65. I hold this view with less confidence than my other pitches, because Palantir has made a habit of beating forecasts, including mine, and I would rather say that plainly than pretend the model settles it.

What would change the view

Guidance for 2027 that implies growth above 60%, signings that start growing with revenue again, especially U.S. commercial deal value still to be delivered, and a GAAP operating margin that holds near 47% while stock pay falls as a share of revenue. Any two of these would move my base case toward the bull case.
