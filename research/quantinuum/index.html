Quantinuum: a lottery ticket priced about right

Quantinuum has $32 million of revenue, $1.9 billion of cash and some of the best quantum hardware anyone has built. Valuing it means pricing a story with a real chance of failure. I get about $26 a share, close to the price. Hold.

Quantinuum went public on June 4 at $60 a share, the first initial public offering of a quantum computing company, and closed on November 20 at $27.74. In between, it reported that its Helios system had set a record for the accuracy of error-corrected operations, raised its revenue outlook, and signed a deal with Oracle. The stock fell anyway, with everything else that is priced on a distant future. I find young companies like this the most interesting to value and the easiest to get wrong, because there are almost no historical numbers to lean on. What you have instead is a story, and the discipline is to turn that story into revenues, margins and reinvestment, and then to be honest about the chance that it doesn't happen at all. That is what I do here, in the same five steps I use for every company.

1. The industry: a market that is mostly still a forecast

A quantum computer stores information in qubits, which can do certain calculations, in chemistry, materials and optimization, that classical computers struggle with. The problem is that qubits are fragile and make errors. The fix is error correction: grouping many physical qubits into one "logical" qubit that catches its own mistakes. A machine that can do this reliably enough to run long computations is called fault-tolerant, and nobody has built a useful one yet. Quantinuum traps charged atoms, or ions, in electromagnetic fields, the same broad approach as IonQ; others use superconducting circuits (IBM, Google, Rigetti), neutral atoms or, in D-Wave's case, a specialized technique called annealing.

McKinsey estimates that quantum computing companies earned a little over $1 billion of revenue in 2025, that this could reach $4.4 billion by 2028, and that the quantum computing market could be worth $43 billion to $71 billion by 2035. I treat those numbers as one informed guess, not a fact, and the industry's own leaders are careful too: on his November earnings call, Rigetti's chief executive said no quantum company can yet show a return over classical computing. For now, revenue comes mostly from governments, research labs and large companies buying access to learn the technology.

2. The business: Helios today, Sol next, Apollo in 2029

Quantinuum was formed in 2021 by combining Honeywell's quantum hardware business with Cambridge Quantum, a software company. It earns most of its revenue from access to its machines, through its own cloud and partners, and from research and services. Its roadmap has three steps: Helios, available now; Sol, which management says is on track for the second half of 2027; and Apollo, a fully fault-tolerant machine targeted for 2029. Customers include BMW, Airbus, JPMorgan Chase, HSBC, Mitsui and Thales.

The numbers that matter more than revenue right now are bookings, cash and the cost of staying in the race. Third-quarter bookings were $98 million, including the Oracle deal, bringing the year to about $104 million, which is why management expects revenue to more than double in 2027. Research and development spending was $97.5 million in the quarter, more than double a year earlier. The company had $1.94 billion of cash and no debt at the end of September, after using $230 million in operations and $65 million on capital spending in the first nine months.

One structural point trips up a lot of quick analysis. Quantinuum has 45.6 million Class A shares, which trade, and 220.3 million Class B shares held by earlier owners, including Honeywell, which own the operating business through a holding company and can swap into Class A shares. Count only the Class A shares and you get a market value of about $1.3 billion; count all 265.8 million and you get $7.4 billion. The second is the right number, and it is the one I use.

3. The model: what has to happen

For a company like this, the model is a story about scale. Management guides to $31 million to $34 million of revenue in 2026 and more than 100% growth in 2027. Beyond that, the question is how big the market gets and how much of it Quantinuum wins. In my base case, it reaches $4.5 billion of revenue in 2036, between 6% and 10% of McKinsey's 2035 range for the whole market, with growth slowing every year from more than 100% in 2028.

Two things in that table deserve attention. First, the cumulative cash burn through 2032 is about $3 billion, more than the $1.94 billion in the bank, so even in a world where everything goes to plan, Quantinuum will probably need to raise more money and dilute today's shareholders. Second, a 37% operating margin in 2036 is generous for a company that will still be building hardware. I allow it because the business, if it wins, will look more like a platform than a machine shop.

The discounted cash flow valuation. Damodaran's approach to young companies is to value the business as if it succeeds and then separately allow for the chance that it fails, rather than burying that chance in a sky-high discount rate. I follow it here.

Read backwards, the price says something specific. If you give Quantinuum a 30% chance of failure and ignore the bull case, $27.74 needs about $6 billion of revenue in 2036. If you are sure it survives, the price needs about $4.7 billion, close to my base case. The disagreement, in other words, is less about how big the company gets than about how likely it is to get there at all.

Trading comparables. Multiples don't mean much for a company that has barely started selling, but they show where the market is. On November 20, Quantinuum traded at about 250 times its trailing 12-month revenue of $29.3 million, against roughly 190 times for D-Wave and about 40 times for IonQ in early November. Applying the range between IonQ and D-Wave to Quantinuum's revenue gives $4 to $21 a share. The market is paying more for Quantinuum's revenue than for anyone else's, and the premium is for its technology.

Precedent transactions. The closest precedents are Quantinuum's own: a $10 billion valuation before its September 2025 round, about $40 a share on today's share count, and the $60 IPO price. Both were set by investors with access to the same story and a much rosier market for long-duration assets.

5. The risks, as three outcomes

For a company at this stage, the risks are not a list of things that could shave a few dollars off the value. They decide whether there is a business at all. Sol or Apollo could slip, a rival approach could reach fault tolerance first, or customers could keep experimenting without ever buying at scale. Against that, Quantinuum has the technical record, the cash to fund about three more years of losses, and a large, patient shareholder in Honeywell. I model three outcomes: failure, where shareholders get back about half of today's cash; success at my base scale; and success at the bull scale.

My take

Quantinuum is a lottery ticket, and at $27.74 it is priced about right for one. My probability-weighted value of about $26 is within 7% of the price, which is well inside the error of any valuation of a company with $32 million of revenue. My rating is Hold. If I owned it, I would keep the position small, because a 30% chance of losing most of the investment is not something to concentrate in. I would add below $20, where the price covers my base case even after allowing for failure, and the bull case comes free. The same arithmetic matters for Honeywell, which owns about 47% of the company: at my value, its stake is worth about $3.3 billion, a little less than the market says and well below the $7.3 billion on Honeywell's balance sheet.

What would change the view

Sol delivered on schedule in the second half of 2027 with performance that matches the roadmap, or 2027 revenue well above double 2026, would lower my failure probability and raise the value. A slip in Sol, a large share sale at a low price, or a rival demonstrating fault tolerance first would do the opposite.
